# Internships, Co-ops, and How to Actually Get One

Internships and co-ops are the highest-octane version of this whole plan: paid,
credentialed, engineering-adjacent work that converts directly into full-time
offers. This guide covers what they are, the one gate that controls access to
most of them, and the paths that are open **right now** to a high school graduate.

## The vocabulary

| Term | What it means | Typical length | Paid? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Internship** | A temporary position, usually summer, doing junior-level real work | 10–12 weeks | Engineering ones almost always pay (often $18–30+/hr) |
| **Co-op** | A formal partnership between a college and employers: you alternate full-time school semesters and full-time paid work semesters, or work part-time year-round | 3–12 months per rotation, often multiple rotations | Yes, and often better than internships |
| **Pre-apprenticeship** | A short program (weeks to a few months) that preps you for and feeds into a registered apprenticeship | 4–16 weeks | Sometimes paid, usually free to attend |
| **Youth/registered apprenticeship** | Paid job + structured training from day one (see the education-pathways guide) | 1–5 years | Yes, from day one |

## The gate nobody tells you about

**Most engineering internships and co-ops require you to be a currently enrolled
student.** Not a graduate, not "planning to enroll" — enrolled. It's how companies
filter, it's how their intern programs are legally structured, and it's why a
motivated 18-year-old applying to "Engineering Intern — Summer 2027" postings with
no enrollment gets auto-rejected while a mediocre sophomore gets an interview.

**The move this implies:** enrolling at community college — even one or two evening
classes while working — flips the switch. The day you have a student ID and an
expected graduation date:

- You can put "Pre-engineering student, [College], expected [year]" on your resume
- You qualify for **Handshake** (the college-recruiting platform nearly every school
  uses — ask the college's career center for access; local employers post there
  specifically to find students like you, with far less competition than Indeed)
- The college's **career center and co-op office** start working for you for free —
  ask directly: "which local companies take co-op students from here?" They keep a list.
- Engineering internship postings stop auto-rejecting you

This is worth saying plainly: **one $200 evening class can unlock a $25/hr summer
internship.** Enrollment is not just degree progress — it's a key.

## What's open BEFORE enrollment (right now, with a HS diploma)

1. **Registered apprenticeships and pre-apprenticeships** — no enrollment needed;
   apprenticeship.gov by ZIP, IBEW locals, and community colleges (ask who sponsors
   "mechatronics" or "advanced manufacturing" students).
2. **Manufacturing summer/seasonal hires at local firms** — small manufacturers
   staff up seasonally; a summer as a production helper at an engineering firm IS
   an internship in everything but the title. Week 3's company census is the target list.
3. **The self-made internship** (see the pitch below) — small companies don't have
   intern *programs*, which means they also don't have intern *requirements*. The
   owner can just say yes.
4. **State workforce programs** — many states run paid youth/young-adult work
   programs (search "[your state] youth apprenticeship" and "[your state] workforce
   development young adult"); your local American Job Center (careeronestop.org
   finds it) can enroll you in some of these for free.
5. **A regular technician job** — never forget that the Week 2/3 technician path
   *is* the working person's internship: paid, full-time, and it doesn't end in August.

## The self-made internship pitch

Small machine shops, controls integrators, and repair outfits (5–50 people) rarely
post internships but often say yes to this, in person or by short email, addressed
to the owner or shop manager:

> "I'm starting an engineering degree at [college] in [month]. Before that, I want
> real shop experience more than I want a mall job. Would you take me on for the
> summer — sweeping, deburring, organizing stock, whatever's useful — so I can
> learn how a real shop runs? I'm reliable and I'll show up every day."

Why it works: it asks for the bottom rung explicitly, signals reliability (their
actual biggest hiring problem), and has an end date so it's low-risk for them.
Ten asks will get a yes. That yes goes on the resume as
*"Shop Assistant, [Company] — supported machinists in a production environment."*

## Once enrolled: playing the internship game properly

- **Timeline is everything.** Summer engineering internships at big companies open
  applications the **August–October before** and are largely filled by February.
  Mark the calendar; "I'll apply in spring" means "next year."
- **Where:** Handshake first (least competition, local employers), then company
  careers pages directly (defense contractors, utilities, and manufacturers from
  the Week 3 census all run intern programs), then Indeed/LinkedIn with
  "engineering intern" + your area.
- **Community college students CAN get these.** Big employers (especially defense
  and semiconductors) explicitly recruit community college and engineering-tech
  students for technician internships. The GPA + a technician work history from
  this program beats a university sophomore's empty resume more often than you'd think.
- **Co-op vs. internship when transferring:** when Week 5's college research
  happens, ask each program: "do you have a co-op program, and which employers
  take your students?" A school with a real co-op pipeline is worth ranking higher —
  co-op students routinely graduate with 12+ months of paid engineering experience
  and a standing job offer.
- **The referral shortcut still rules.** A Week 7 contact saying "we take interns,
  send me your resume" skips the entire pile. Every informational interview should
  end with: *"Does your company take interns or co-op students? Who runs that?"*

## How this fits the 8-week program

- **Week 3:** when building the openings list, also flag any company that mentions
  interns, co-ops, or apprentices anywhere on its careers page.
- **Week 5:** ask every college about co-op programs and Handshake access; ask the
  community college which employers sponsor students.
- **Week 7:** add the intern question to every interview (wording above).
- **Week 8:** the five-year plan should name the first internship/co-op window
  you'll be eligible for and the date applications open for it.
